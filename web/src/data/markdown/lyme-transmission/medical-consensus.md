# Topic 11: How Lyme Is Transmitted — Medical Consensus

> Scope note: This topic asks whether *Borrelia* travels only by tick bite, or
> also **vertically (mother to child, in utero)** and **sexually**. It is
> personally consequential for people who have Lyme but no remembered tick bite,
> or whose family members are also affected. The mainstream answer is layered —
> it accepts more than the loudest headlines suggest and less than the patient
> community hopes. The minority case and the congenital-syphilis analogy are in
> `non-consensus.md`.

## Overview: the mainstream answer is three-tiered

1. **Tick bite — ESTABLISHED and dominant.** The overwhelming, evidence-based
   route is the bite of an infected blacklegged tick (*Ixodes scapularis* /
   *I. pacificus* in North America; *I. ricinus* in Europe). The CDC and all
   guideline bodies treat this as essentially the route of human infection
   [T11-S001].
2. **Mother-to-fetus (transplacental) — BIOLOGICALLY POSSIBLE, RARE, and
   contested as a *syndrome*.** Mainstream sources acknowledge that *B.
   burgdorferi* **can** cross the placenta — it was documented in 1985 in an
   untreated mother whose fetus died with spirochetes in multiple organs
   [T11-S002]. Adverse pregnancy outcomes are associated mainly with **untreated**
   maternal Lyme [T11-S003][T11-S004]. **However**, a defined "congenital Lyme
   disease" syndrome is **not** established; it was **removed from ICD-11** after
   systematic review (NICE, supported by CDC), and with appropriate antibiotic
   treatment in pregnancy adverse outcomes are uncommon [T11-S005].
3. **Sexual / person-to-person — NOT SUPPORTED.** The CDC states there is no
   credible evidence Lyme is sexually transmitted; the spirochete's biology and
   animal studies do not support it, and there is no evidence of transmission via
   blood transfusion of treated donors, casual contact, or breast milk
   [T11-S001][T11-S005].

A crucial, compassionate clarification: **a missing tick-bite history is normal,
not evidence of an exotic route.** *Ixodes* nymphs are poppy-seed-sized and their
bite is painless; most people with Lyme never recall a bite. So "I was never
bitten" does not by itself imply congenital or sexual acquisition.

## Established mechanisms (mainstream)

### Tick-borne transmission
*Borrelia* cycles between rodent/bird reservoirs and *Ixodes* ticks; humans are
incidental hosts. Transmission generally requires the tick to be attached and
feeding for a period (often cited as ~24–48 hours), during which spirochetes
migrate from the tick midgut to the host [T11-S001].

### Transplacental (vertical) transmission — what is accepted
- The spirochete **can** cross the placenta; Schlesinger's 1985 autopsy case
  found *B. burgdorferi* in fetal liver, spleen, bone marrow, heart, brain, and
  kidney after untreated first-trimester maternal Lyme [T11-S002].
- Systematic review finds **maternal Lyme, especially untreated, is associated
  with adverse outcomes** (fetal death, prematurity, rash), but the evidence is
  limited and confounded, and **no consistent congenital malformation syndrome**
  has been proven [T11-S003][T11-S004].
- **Appropriate antibiotic treatment during pregnancy markedly reduces risk**;
  this is why guidelines emphasize prompt treatment of maternal Lyme [T11-S003].

### Why mainstream rejects a defined congenital syndrome
- Systematic reviews (NICE) found the evidence insufficient to define congenital
  Lyme disease as an entity; **ICD-11 removed the category** [T11-S005].
- Many reported cases lacked controls, confirmed maternal infection, or excluded
  other causes.

### Why mainstream rejects sexual transmission
- The spirochete is adapted to the tick–mammal cycle, not genital mucosa; it does
  not sustain the kind of bacteremia/genital shedding that drives sexual
  transmission of *Treponema* [T11-S001].
- No epidemiologic signal: Lyme does not cluster like an STI, and partners of
  patients are not at elevated risk in population data [T11-S005].

## Diagnostic / clinical implications (mainstream)

- Pregnant patients with Lyme should be **treated promptly** with
  pregnancy-safe antibiotics (not doxycycline); this is the key actionable point
  and is not controversial [T11-S003].
- A child of a mother with treated Lyme is **not** presumed congenitally infected;
  testing is guided by clinical findings.
- "No tick bite" should prompt consideration that the bite was simply unnoticed,
  not automatic suspicion of congenital/sexual routes [T11-S001].

## Open questions within mainstream

- **How often does transplacental transmission actually occur, and when it does,
  does it cause disease in the child?** The honest mainstream answer is *rarely,
  and unclear* — better prospective data are explicitly called for
  [T11-S003][T11-S005].
- **Could under-recognized maternal-fetal transmission explain some "no-bite"
  cases?** Acknowledged as unresolved, not dismissed [T11-S005].
- The role of **co-infections** (e.g., *Babesia*) in vertical transmission is even
  less studied.
