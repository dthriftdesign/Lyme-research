# Topic 6: Lyme, Parasitic Co-infection, and Suicide — Non-Consensus / Minority Research

> **Crisis resources.** US — call/text 988; UK — Samaritans 116 123;
> international — findahelpline.com. Public-health framing only; no methods detail.
>
> Flags: `mainstream` · `minority-research` · `fringe` · `historical` ·
> `speculative`. Inclusion is not endorsement.

## Overview

The central non-consensus claim, advanced primarily by **Robert Bransfield**, is
that **Lyme and associated diseases (LAD) are a meaningful, under-recognized
contributor to suicide**, operating through infection-driven neuroinflammation
and the same kynurenine/quinolinic-acid mechanisms that mainstream neuroscience
links to suicidality [T6-S001]. The handoff is explicit that this should be
treated as the **serious public-health research question it is**, not dismissed.

## Bransfield's suicidality thesis — `minority-research`

Bransfield's 2017 paper argues that multiple infections and their pro-inflammatory
cytokines, inflammatory-metabolic changes, and **quinolinic-acid/glutamate**
alterations modify neural circuits to increase suicidality, and that a similar
pathophysiology operates in LAD [T6-S001]. He estimates a non-trivial annual
suicide burden attributable to LAD. Assessment: the **mechanism is mainstream**
[T6-S004][T6-S005]; the **infection-specific attribution and the magnitude
estimates** are `minority-research` — extrapolated from clinical experience and
mechanism rather than from controlled epidemiology. The direction of the claim is
plausible; the numbers are uncertain.

## Suicidality/aggression after Lyme — `minority-research`/`speculative`

Related work (e.g., "suicidal and homicidal tendencies after Lyme disease")
extends the thesis to externalizing behavior and aggression [T6-S002]. This is
more `speculative`: case-series and clinical-impression based, vulnerable to
selection and attribution bias, and easily sensationalized. It is included
because the underlying neuro-immune mechanism is real, but the behavioral claims
outrun the evidence and must be stated cautiously to avoid stigmatizing patients.

## The co-infection severity link — `minority-research`

A specific, defensible sub-claim: because *Babesia* and multi-pathogen
co-infection increase illness severity and duration [T6-S007], they should
increase the chronic-illness suicide-risk load. This follows straightforwardly
from established chronic-illness risk [T6-S006] and does not require an
infection-specific brain effect — arguably the **strongest and least
controversial** part of the topic.

## Why mainstream is cautious (stated fairly)

- **Confounding:** severe chronic illness raises suicide risk regardless of cause;
  isolating an infection-specific effect is methodologically hard [T6-S006].
- **Attribution and selection bias** in clinical cohorts of patients who already
  attribute their suffering to Lyme.
- **Risk of harm both ways:** under-recognition delays mental-health care;
  over-attribution ("your suicidality is just the infection") can also delay
  evidence-based psychiatric treatment.

## Honest epistemic assessment

- **Inflammation/kynurenine → suicidality** — `mainstream` [T6-S004][T6-S005].
- **Severe chronic Lyme/co-infection raises suicide-risk load** — `mainstream`-to-
  `minority-research`, well-grounded [T6-S006][T6-S007].
- **Lyme-specific, quantified suicide burden** (Bransfield's magnitude) —
  `minority-research`: plausible, mechanism-backed, not epidemiologically
  established [T6-S001].
- **Infection-driven homicidality/aggression** — `speculative`; handle with
  particular care against sensationalism [T6-S002].

## Named researchers

Robert Bransfield (the principal voice on Lyme and suicide; `minority-research`).
