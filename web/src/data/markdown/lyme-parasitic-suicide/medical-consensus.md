# Topic 6: Lyme, Parasitic Co-infection, and Suicide — Medical Consensus

> 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline); UK — Samaritans 116 123; international —
> findahelpline.com. This file treats suicide as a **public-health and
> neuroscience research question**, per the project's handling rules. It contains
> **no methods detail** and is not clinical guidance.

## Overview

This topic asks whether **Lyme disease (and its co-infections, including the
parasite *Babesia*) is associated with elevated suicide risk**, and through what
mechanisms. The mainstream framing has two well-established pillars and one
contested bridge:

1. **Chronic, painful, disabling, stigmatized illness raises suicide risk** —
   this is solidly established for chronic illness generally and applies to
   severe/persistent Lyme and co-infection illness as a matter of base rates
   [T6-S006].
2. **Neuroinflammation and the kynurenine/quinolinic-acid pathway are mechanistically
   linked to suicidality** — a robust and growing mainstream neuroscience
   literature independent of Lyme [T6-S004][T6-S005].
3. **The contested bridge:** that *Borrelia*/co-infection specifically drives
   suicidality via these mechanisms at a population-relevant scale. This is argued
   most forcefully by Bransfield (see `non-consensus.md`) and is plausible but not
   established as a quantified, infection-specific effect [T6-S001].

## Established Mechanisms (mainstream)

### Inflammation → suicidality
Pro-inflammatory cytokines and immune activation are associated with suicidal
behavior across diagnoses. A leading mechanism is the **kynurenine pathway**:
inflammation shifts tryptophan metabolism toward **quinolinic acid**, an NMDA-
receptor agonist and excitotoxin elevated in the CSF of suicide attempters, while
reducing neuroprotective metabolites [T6-S004][T6-S005]. This is a general
psychoneuroimmunology finding, not Lyme-specific, but it is exactly the pathway
any infection-suicide hypothesis would invoke.

### Chronic illness, pain, and disability
Independent of any direct brain effect, chronic pain, fatigue, sleep disruption,
loss of function, diagnostic uncertainty, and medical invalidation are recognized
suicide risk factors. Severe and prolonged Lyme/co-infection illness (worse with
*Babesia* co-infection [T6-S007]) concentrates these risk factors [T6-S006].

### Direct neuroborreliosis effects
Acute CNS infection can produce mood disturbance, emotional dysregulation, and —
in rare documented cases — suicidality that improved with treatment of the
infection [T6-S003]. Mainstream medicine accepts neuropsychiatric symptoms in
active neuroborreliosis; it is the chronic/persistent attribution that is
debated.

## Diagnostic / Clinical Approach (mainstream)

- **Screen for suicide risk** in patients with severe, chronic, or
  neuropsychiatrically-symptomatic Lyme/co-infection illness, as for any
  high-burden chronic disease.
- Treat documented infection and co-infection (including *Babesia*) and comorbid
  depression/anxiety on their own merits.
- Provide crisis resources and mental-health linkage; do not frame psychiatric
  symptoms as "just infection" in a way that delays mental-health care.

## Open Questions Within Mainstream

- **Is there an infection-specific suicidality signal** beyond the expected
  chronic-illness elevation? Hard to isolate from confounders [T6-S001].
- **Does treating infection reduce suicidality** beyond treating depression? Not
  established by controlled data.
- **Co-infection contribution:** does *Babesia*/multi-infection severity
  [T6-S007] translate into measurable suicide-risk elevation? Unquantified.
