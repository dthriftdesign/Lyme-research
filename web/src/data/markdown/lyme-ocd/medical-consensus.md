# Topic 4: Lyme and OCD — Medical Consensus

> Scope: what mainstream neurology/psychiatry accepts about Lyme disease and
> obsessive-compulsive symptomatology. The stronger infection-causes-OCD claims
> (Bransfield, case-report literature) are developed in `non-consensus.md`.

## Overview

Mainstream medicine accepts that **neuroborreliosis** (CNS *Borrelia* infection)
can produce neuropsychiatric symptoms, and that Lyme disease has a recognized
**neuropsychiatric dimension** — historically catalogued by Fallon & Nields and
others as including mood disturbance, cognitive slowing ("Lyme encephalopathy"),
irritability, anxiety, and, less commonly, obsessions/compulsions, psychosis, or
panic [T4-S001][T4-S002]. **OCD as a primary or isolated presentation of Lyme is
rare and rests largely on case reports**, not controlled data [T4-S001]. The
consensus position: neuropsychiatric symptoms are real in active CNS infection
and generally improve with antibiotic treatment of that infection; a distinct
"chronic Lyme causes OCD" entity is not established.

The mechanistic bridge that makes the hypothesis biologically plausible is the
**post-infectious autoimmune basal-ganglia model** established for
streptococcus — PANDAS/PANS — in which infection triggers anti-neuronal
autoantibodies and basal-ganglia dysfunction producing acute-onset OCD and tics
[T4-S005]. Whether *Borrelia* can do the same is the open question.

## Established Mechanisms

### Direct CNS infection (neuroborreliosis)
In acute/early neuroborreliosis, spirochetal invasion of the CNS produces
meningitis, cranial neuritis, radiculopathy and, less often, encephalitis with
behavioral change. Neuropsychiatric symptoms in this setting are attributed to
direct infection plus the inflammatory response, and respond to standard IV
antibiotics [T4-S002][T4-S007].

### Neuroinflammation and "Lyme encephalopathy"
A milder cognitive/affective syndrome (memory, concentration, mood) is described
in some treated patients, linked to cytokine-mediated neuroinflammation rather
than active brain infection. Its status and persistence are debated and overlap
with post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome [T4-S006][T4-S007].

### Autoimmune basal-ganglia model (borrowed from PANDAS/PANS)
The best-characterized infection→OCD mechanism is **molecular mimicry**:
anti-streptococcal antibodies cross-react with basal-ganglia neurons, altering
CaMKII signaling and dopaminergic circuits to produce abrupt OCD/tics
[T4-S005]. This is mainstream for streptococcus. Its extension to *Borrelia* is
hypothesized (case reports invoking "Lyme-triggered PANS") but not established
[T4-S004].

## Diagnostic Approach (mainstream)

- New-onset OCD does **not** warrant routine Lyme testing in the absence of
  exposure history or other Lyme features; pretest probability is low and false
  positives cause harm.
- Where there is genuine exposure, a compatible illness, and objective CNS
  findings, evaluate for neuroborreliosis with serology and, if indicated, CSF
  analysis (including CSF:serum antibody index) [T4-S007].
- Standard psychiatric assessment for OCD; consider PANS/PANDAS workup in
  abrupt-onset pediatric cases [T4-S005].

## Treatment Consensus (mainstream)

- Treat **documented neuroborreliosis** with guideline antibiotics; associated
  neuropsychiatric symptoms often improve [T4-S007].
- Treat **OCD** on its own merits — SSRIs and exposure-and-response-prevention
  (ERP) — regardless of any presumed infectious trigger.
- Mainstream guidance does **not** support prolonged antibiotics for OCD
  attributed to "chronic Lyme."

## Open Questions Within Mainstream

- **Can *Borrelia* trigger a PANS-like autoimmune OCD** analogous to
  streptococcus? Biologically plausible, not demonstrated [T4-S004][T4-S005].
- **How much of the neuropsychiatric burden** in long-standing Lyme is infection,
  post-infectious neuroinflammation, or the psychological toll of chronic illness
  [T4-S006]?
- **Biomarkers** to distinguish infection-driven from primary OCD are lacking.
