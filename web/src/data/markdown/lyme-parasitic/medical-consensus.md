# Topic 5: Lyme and Parasitic Infections — Medical Consensus

> Scope: "parasitic" here centers on **Babesia** (a protozoan parasite and the
> principal *bona fide* parasitic co-infection of Lyme), with attention to the
> boundary between real tick-borne co-infection and the broader, contested
> "chronic-Lyme + parasites" claims handled in `non-consensus.md`.

## Overview

The mainstream, well-evidenced parasitic intersection with Lyme disease is
**babesiosis** — infection with the intra-erythrocytic protozoan *Babesia
microti* (and related species), transmitted by the **same *Ixodes scapularis*
tick** that transmits *Borrelia burgdorferi*. Because one tick can carry both,
**concurrent Lyme + babesiosis is the most common tick-borne co-infection in the
United States**, and co-infection is clinically meaningful: it produces **more
intense and more prolonged illness** than Lyme alone [T5-S001][T5-S002]. This is
settled, guideline-level medicine. Beyond *Babesia*, mainstream tick-borne
co-infections are mostly bacterial (Anaplasma, *Bartonella*-debated, Borrelia
miyamotoi); claims of widespread helminth/novel-protozoan co-infection in chronic
Lyme are not mainstream and are addressed separately.

## Established Mechanisms

### Shared vector, frequent co-transmission
*I. scapularis* nymphs can be simultaneously infected with *B. burgdorferi* and
*B. microti*; in endemic regions roughly **20% of Lyme patients report concurrent
babesiosis and up to ~25% of babesiosis patients have Lyme** [T5-S002]. Ecology
and reservoir overlap (white-footed mouse) drive co-occurrence [T5-S005].

### Additive/synergistic disease severity
The landmark Krause study showed co-infected patients have more symptoms (fatigue,
chills, sweats, headache, anorexia, emotional lability, nausea, splenomegaly) and
far longer illness — **~50% symptomatic ≥3 months vs ~4% with Lyme alone**
[T5-S001]. Animal models support increased severity and altered immune responses
in co-infection [T5-S003]. Recent large cohort data associate *Babesia*
co-infection with worse outcomes including mortality signals in vulnerable groups
[T5-S004].

### Babesia pathophysiology
*Babesia* parasitizes red cells, causing a malaria-like hemolytic illness
(fever, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia), severe in the asplenic,
immunocompromised, and elderly. It is diagnosed by blood smear, PCR, and
serology — **distinct from Lyme diagnostics** — which matters because Lyme
treatment (doxycycline) does **not** treat *Babesia* [T5-S004].

## Diagnostic Approach (mainstream)

- In a Lyme patient with disproportionate fever, sweats, hemolytic anemia, or
  thrombocytopenia, **test for babesiosis** (smear, *Babesia* PCR, serology)
  [T5-S004].
- Co-infection should be considered when Lyme symptoms are unusually severe or
  fail to improve on appropriate Lyme therapy [T5-S001].
- Anaplasmosis and *Borrelia miyamotoi* are other co-transmitted considerations.

## Treatment Consensus (mainstream)

- **Babesiosis:** atovaquone + azithromycin (mild–moderate) or clindamycin +
  quinine (severe); exchange transfusion in severe cases. Requires
  parasite-directed therapy distinct from antibiotics for *Borrelia* [T5-S004].
- **Lyme:** standard regimens as per the 2020 guideline.
- Co-infected patients need **both** treatments; missing *Babesia* is a common
  cause of "treatment-resistant Lyme" that is actually untreated babesiosis.

## Epidemiology

Babesiosis is expanding in range with *I. scapularis*, concentrated in the
Northeast and upper Midwest, increasingly recognized nationally; co-infection
rates rise with local *Babesia* prevalence [T5-S002][T5-S004].

## Open Questions Within Mainstream

- **Persistence of *Babesia*** in immunocompetent hosts and its contribution to
  lingering symptoms is debated.
- **How often "chronic Lyme" is actually undiagnosed/under-treated babesiosis**
  is unquantified but clinically important [T5-S001].
- The pathophysiology of **co-infection synergy** (immune modulation by one
  pathogen worsening the other) is still being mapped [T5-S003].
