# Topic 1: Lyme and Arthritis — Medical Consensus

> Scope note: This file synthesizes mainstream, peer-reviewed and
> guideline-level understanding of Lyme arthritis. Minority and contested
> positions live in `non-consensus.md`. Every claim carries an inline source ID

## Overview

Lyme arthritis is the most common late manifestation of *Borrelia burgdorferi*
infection in North America and was the clinical syndrome through which the
disease was first recognized — the 1975–1977 cluster of oligoarticular
arthritis in children around Lyme, Connecticut [T1-S012]. Untreated, arthritis
develops in roughly 60% of patients infected with *B. burgdorferi* sensu
stricto, typically months after the tick bite, presenting as intermittent or
persistent monoarticular or oligoarticular swelling that overwhelmingly favors
large joints — the knee above all others [T1-S002][T1-S013].

The modern consensus frames Lyme arthritis as a two-phase disease. In the
**infectious phase**, live spirochetes in the joint drive synovitis that
responds to antibiotics in the large majority of patients. In a minority, a
**post-infectious / antibiotic-refractory phase** persists after spirochetal
killing, now understood as a dysregulated, partly autoimmune inflammatory
process rather than ongoing active infection [T1-S017][T1-S004]. This
infection-to-autoimmunity model is the central organizing idea of current
mainstream pathogenesis research and is led largely by the Steere group at
Massachusetts General Hospital.

## Established Mechanisms

### Spirochetal arthritogenesis
Arthritis development depends partly on spirochetal genotype. Strains with
particular ribosomal spacer (RST1) and *ospC* types are more arthritogenic and
more often associated with hematogenous dissemination and joint involvement
[T1-S001][T1-S017]. The spirochete reaches the joint hematogenously; its
peptidoglycan and lipoproteins are potent innate-immune stimulants
[T1-S009].

### Th1/Th17 inflammatory signature
Synovial fluid in active Lyme arthritis shows a strong T-helper-1 and
T-helper-17 cytokine profile — elevated CCL2/3/4, TNF-α, CXCL9, CXCL10, IL-6,
IL-8, IL-1β, IL-17, IL-23 and IFN-γ. The magnitude of this response, especially
high IFN-γ relative to regulatory signals, correlates with antibiotic-refractory
course [T1-S003][T1-S017].

### HLA-restricted autoimmunity
A genetic susceptibility marker for refractory disease is the carriage of
certain HLA-DR molecules that bind an OspA epitope; ~79% of antibiotic-refractory
patients carry at least one of seven OspA-binding DR alleles versus ~46% of
antibiotic-responsive patients [T1-S003]. This was an early clue that adaptive
immunity to spirochetal antigens cross-reacts with host tissue.

### Identified human autoantigens
The Steere group has now identified specific self-antigens targeted by T and B
cells in refractory disease, the strongest mechanistic evidence for
infection-induced autoimmunity in any human Lyme syndrome:
- **Endothelial cell growth factor (ECGF / thymidine phosphorylase)** — the
  first identified autoantigen; antibody responses associate with obliterative
  microvascular lesions in refractory synovium [T1-S004][T1-S005].
- **Annexin A2**, **matrix metalloproteinase-10**, **apolipoprotein B-100** and
  several **synovial extracellular-matrix proteins** (e.g., fibronectin-1,
  laminin) are additional targets, linking the immune response to the vascular
  damage and fibroblast proliferation seen histologically [T1-S006][T1-S007].

### Persistent antigen, not persistent infection
Two strands of evidence explain why inflammation can outlast viable spirochetes.
In a murine model, intravital microscopy showed *B. burgdorferi* antigen
deposits remaining adjacent to cartilage for weeks after antibiotics had cleared
live organisms [T1-S008]. In humans, *B. burgdorferi* **peptidoglycan** — a
cell-wall fragment the spirochete cannot fully recycle — was detected in the
synovial fluid of refractory patients and is capable of driving ongoing
inflammation as a retained, slowly-cleared antigen [T1-S009]. Mainstream
interpretation: residual antigen plus host autoimmunity, not live infection,
sustains refractory arthritis.

## Diagnostic Approach

- **Clinical picture:** mono/oligoarthritis of a large joint (knee in ~90%),
  often with large effusions disproportionate to pain [T1-S002].
- **Serology:** standard two-tier testing (EIA then immunoblot, or modified
  two-EIA). Virtually all patients with Lyme arthritis are strongly
  seropositive with expanded IgG immunoblot banding; a negative IgG serology
  effectively excludes the diagnosis [T1-S001][T1-S002].
- **Synovial fluid PCR** for *B. burgdorferi* DNA can support the diagnosis but
  positivity falls after antibiotic therapy and does not by itself indicate live
  organisms [T1-S001].
- Two-tier serology cannot distinguish active from past infection (antibodies
  persist for years), so diagnosis is clinical plus serologic context
  [T1-S001].

## Treatment Consensus (2020 IDSA/AAN/ACR)

1. **Initial therapy:** a 28-day course of oral doxycycline (or amoxicillin /
   cefuroxime) for Lyme arthritis [T1-S001].
2. **Partial or no response:** a second 28-day oral course, or a 2–4 week course
   of IV ceftriaxone [T1-S001].
3. **Antibiotic-refractory arthritis** (persistent synovitis after oral + IV
   antibiotics): manage as immune-mediated with DMARDs (methotrexate,
   hydroxychloroquine), intra-articular steroids, and — in resistant cases —
   arthroscopic synovectomy, which is frequently curative [T1-S001][T1-S017].
4. The guidelines explicitly recommend **against** prolonged or repeated
   antibiotic courses for arthritis that has failed standard therapy, on the
   basis that refractory disease is post-infectious [T1-S001].

## Epidemiology

Lyme arthritis is concentrated in the US Northeast and upper Midwest, tracking
*Ixodes scapularis* range, and is far less common in Europe, where *B. afzelii*
and *B. garinii* predominate and produce more cutaneous and neurologic disease.
With earlier diagnosis and treatment of erythema migrans, the incidence of
arthritis as a presenting late manifestation has declined over recent decades
[T1-S002][T1-S013].

## Open Questions Within Mainstream

- **What proportion of "refractory" cases reflect undetected residual infection
  vs. pure autoimmunity?** The dominant view is post-infectious, but mainstream
  authors acknowledge the question is not fully closed [T1-S017][T1-S008].
- **Why do only some HLA-susceptible, highly-inflamed patients progress to
  refractory disease?** Microbiome, obesity and prior immune history are under
  study [T1-S017].
- **Biomarkers** to predict refractory course at presentation remain
  investigational [T1-S003].
- The relationship between Lyme arthritis and **post-treatment Lyme disease
  syndrome (PTLDS)** — a distinct, largely non-articular symptom complex — is
  unsettled; the retreatment trials found no durable benefit of prolonged
  antibiotics for persistent symptoms [T1-S014].
